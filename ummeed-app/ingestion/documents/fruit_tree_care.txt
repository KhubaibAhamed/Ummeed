Fertilizer and Soil Guidance for Mango and Guava Orchards

Mango trees establish best in deep, well-drained loamy soil with a pH between 5.5 and 7.5. Waterlogged or heavy clay soils that hold standing water around the roots are the single biggest cause of young mango tree failure, so drainage matters more for mango than almost any other factor.

For a mature, bearing mango tree, a typical annual fertilizer schedule applies farmyard manure or compost generously once a year after harvest, combined with split doses of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Nitrogen supports vegetative growth but should be reduced in the months leading up to flowering, since excess nitrogen at that stage encourages leafy growth at the expense of flowering and fruit set. Potassium becomes more important during fruit development, since it directly supports fruit size and sweetness. Applying fertilizer in a ring around the drip line of the canopy, rather than right against the trunk, matches where the active feeder roots actually are.

Young mango trees, in their first two to three years, need smaller, more frequent doses of a balanced fertilizer to support steady growth without overwhelming a still-developing root system.

Guava is more tolerant of a wider soil range than mango and can grow in soils from sandy loam to moderately heavy clay, with an ideal pH between 4.5 and 8.2 — one of the widest tolerances among common fruit trees. That said, guava still performs best in well-drained soil with good organic matter content, and like mango, does poorly in soil that stays waterlogged for extended periods.

Guava responds well to farmyard manure applied twice a year, along with nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in split doses timed around its two main flowering and fruiting cycles in most regions. Because guava can flower and fruit more than once a year, fertilizer scheduling is typically tied to which flowering flush the tree is entering rather than a single annual cycle like mango.

For both trees, a soil test before establishing a new orchard is worth the cost — it reveals whether lime is needed to correct acidity, or gypsum to address sodic or saline conditions, before young trees are planted and problems become harder to correct.
