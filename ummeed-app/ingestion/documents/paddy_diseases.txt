Common Paddy (Rice) Diseases and Management

Rice blast, caused by the fungus Magnaporthe oryzae, is one of the most damaging paddy diseases. It appears as diamond-shaped, grayish-white lesions with brown borders on leaves, and can also attack the neck of the panicle, causing entire grain heads to die before filling. Blast spreads fastest in humid weather with heavy dew, especially when nitrogen fertilizer has been applied in excess. Using resistant varieties, avoiding excess nitrogen, and applying a recommended fungicide at the first sign of leaf lesions are the standard responses.

Bacterial leaf blight shows up as yellowish, water-soaked streaks along the leaf margins that turn white to gray as the disease progresses, eventually causing the whole leaf to wilt and dry from the tip downward. It spreads through irrigation water and wind-driven rain, and tends to be worse in fields with standing water and dense planting. Avoiding excessive nitrogen, maintaining proper spacing for airflow, and draining fields periodically instead of keeping continuous standing water can reduce its spread.

Sheath blight appears as oval, grayish-green lesions with irregular borders near the waterline on the leaf sheath, which can climb up the plant in humid, densely planted fields. It thrives where fields stay flooded continuously and canopies are thick. Alternating wetting and drying instead of continuous flooding, along with balanced fertilization rather than heavy nitrogen use, helps keep it in check.

Brown spot appears as small, circular to oval brown lesions scattered across the leaf, often linked to nutrient-deficient soil, particularly low potassium. Improving soil fertility with balanced NPK application and using treated, disease-free seed reduces brown spot incidence significantly.

General preventive steps that help against most paddy diseases: use certified disease-free seed, avoid continuous mono-cropping of paddy in the same field season after season, remove and destroy infected plant debris after harvest rather than leaving it in the field, and apply nitrogen in split doses rather than all at once at planting, since excess nitrogen early in the season makes plants more susceptible to fungal infection.

Farmers noticing unfamiliar lesion patterns or rapid spread across a field should get a sample checked by their local agricultural extension office rather than guessing, since several rice diseases share superficially similar symptoms but need different treatments.
