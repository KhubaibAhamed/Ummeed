Irrigation Guidance for Small and Marginal Farmers

Choosing the right irrigation method depends on your crop, soil type, and water availability. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone through a network of tubes and emitters, and is well suited to high-value crops like vegetables, fruit orchards, and cotton. It reduces water use by thirty to sixty percent compared to flood irrigation and also limits weed growth, since only the root zone stays wet.

Sprinkler irrigation works well for closely spaced crops such as wheat, groundnut, and pulses, and is a good middle option between drip and flood irrigation in terms of both cost and water efficiency.

Flood or furrow irrigation is the traditional method and remains common for paddy, since rice benefits from standing water during much of its growth cycle. However, flood irrigation uses the most water and is prone to uneven distribution across a field, especially where land leveling is poor.

Timing matters as much as method. Watering early in the morning or in the evening reduces water loss to evaporation. For most row crops, allowing the top two to three centimeters of soil to dry out between waterings encourages deeper root growth and reduces the risk of fungal disease at the root.

Farmers with borewells should monitor water table depth over the season, since over-extraction during peak summer months can lower yields in following seasons. Rainwater harvesting structures, even small farm ponds, can supplement borewell water and reduce dependence on erratic electricity supply for pumps.

Signs of both underwatering and overwatering can look similar at first glance — wilting leaves. The key difference: underwatered plants recover within a few hours of watering, while overwatered plants stay wilted even after watering because the roots are waterlogged and cannot take up water properly.
